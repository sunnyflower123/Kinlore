A family's shared memory archive. An old person rambles; the app turns it into structure — and keeps the voice that said it.

The one thing it does

Grandmother presses a big button and rambles for ninety seconds about an old photo. The app returns a structured memory attached to that photo, person cards for the relatives she mentioned, the year and the place — and three follow-up questions back.

The original recording is always kept and can be played back from the memory. Her voice is itself the inheritance, not a step on the way to text.

Why it exists

Grandparents know, but they cannot explain it in a structured way. They do not fill in forms and they do not tag photographs — they talk. Album and genealogy apps demand structured input from the one person who will never produce it, and so the knowledge disappears at the funeral.

What it is built on

  • The primary user is 80 years old. Dynamic Type to the largest size, VoiceOver, 60 pt touch targets. Accessibility is the design, not a checklist bolted to it.
  • Telling a story is never behind the paywall. The paywall limits photographs and AI minutes, never the act of recording a memory.
  • The AI proposes, a human confirms. An inferred relative is created unconfirmed and does not appear in the family tree as fact. A wrong relationship is worse than a missing one.
  • Uncertainty is stored, not rounded off. "Sometime in the fifties" is kept as a range with decade precision, never forced into a date.

Built in the open

Kinlore is a side project for the RevenueCat Shipaton 2026, entered in the Next Gen student category. The source is public, including the reasoning: the plan, the architecture, and the design decisions are all in the repository.

The app follows the phone's language: its interface is English by default, and Finnish on a phone set to Finnish, the language of the 80-year-old it exists for. The documentation, comments and commit messages are English.
